## A map for people who notice things

People often notice a change in a place before it finds its way into a tidy report. A creek runs cloudy after heavy rain. A familiar path dries earlier than it did last year. A roadside reading looks different on the afternoon the traffic was diverted.

Field Index is being developed by a small Canadian collective working across field ecology, mapmaking, community organizing, and software. We came together through a shared interest in waterways, trails, and the public spaces people move through every day.

We’re building a public map for citizen scientists and curious local residents. The aim is simple: make environmental observations easier to explore without stripping away the route, note, time, or condition that helps explain each one. The first public preview is planned for Spring 2027.

## One place to follow the evidence

The main view keeps search, filters, the map, and an observation record together. Someone can start with a place they know, open an individual reading, and then see how it relates to nearby routes or an earlier point in time.

The map stays at the centre of the experience. Controls sit around its edges, and the source record remains close at hand. We want it to feel less like reading a dashboard and more like spreading a map and a set of field notes across the same table.

## Layers that explain what you’re seeing

A dot on a map can look more certain than it really is. Field Index separates the view into a few plain layers so people can see what each part represents. Grid provides a geographic reference. Signals show recorded observations. Routes show how those observations connect across a place. Notes carry the details that do not fit neatly into a measurement.

The small prototype below is one part of the product that already works. Grid and Signals appear first; Routes and Notes can be switched on separately. The controls stay deliberately simple because the important thing is knowing what the map is showing at any moment.

## More detail, without overstating the picture

At a regional scale, showing every available point can turn a map into confetti. It can also make a handful of locations with many samples look like complete coverage. Field Index uses four levels of display detail instead. Moving from 1× to 8× reveals more points from the same sample set; it does not add readings or change the underlying data.

The current multiplier labels are still part of the prototype. Before the public preview, we plan to replace them with language that describes the view more naturally, from a broad regional scan to close local detail. Whatever the final labels become, the map should always make it clear when some observations are being held back from view.

## Built around how people move through a place

Some of the most useful context never fits in a measurement column: recent rain, a closed road, shade along the water, or a note made halfway through a walk. Those details are easy to lose once a field record becomes a row in a larger dataset.

We’re shaping Field Index around a familiar path through the evidence: find a place, open an observation, check where and when it was recorded, then compare it with the wider set. Routes and notes remain visible because they often explain the reading better than another summary number would.

## What we’re working toward

The Spring 2027 preview will focus on one clear loop: find a place, inspect its observations, understand the context around them, and compare what changed. We’re keeping that first release intentionally small while we work through questions about public data coverage, source clarity, and how much detail belongs on the map at once.

Three ideas continue to guide the work: keep the landscape visible, show where the evidence came from, and be honest about what the map cannot say. Field Index is not meant to declare that a place is safe, healthy, or fully understood. It is a way to look more closely and know what you are looking at.
