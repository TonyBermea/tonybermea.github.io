## Selling an object without flattening its character

MCHNS explores commerce for small kinetic toys built from springs, gears, wire, and found mechanical parts. A conventional product grid would make the objects easy to compare, but it would also remove the movement and odd personality that make them interesting.

The prototype starts with discovery rather than inventory. Circular crops act like peepholes into the collection, allowing a visitor to encounter materials and silhouettes before entering the structured shop.

## An entrance that teaches the visual language

The opening screen introduces the repeated circle before asking for a shopping action. That shape becomes a consistent frame for product photography across rooms, related items, and the model catalogue. Large type establishes orientation while the surrounding objects keep the composition playful and slightly unpredictable.

## Mapping rooms, products, and exits

The interaction map connects the entrance to a front page, thematic rooms, single stories, the shop, product details, cart, lookbook, and contact. The network is intentionally not a straight funnel: editorial campaigns can lead into a product, and a product can return a visitor to related objects or a broader collection.

## Campaign-led merchandising

Fun Machines and STEM behave like editorial rooms rather than promotional banners. Each gives the toys a context—movement, making, and playful engineering—before introducing a direct route to the shop. The catalogue remains available, but it is not the only way to understand the collection.

## Product detail as a focused interruption

The Citter state opens above the larger collection rather than replacing it. Background context remains visible, while the central panel concentrates the product image, short origin story, and purchase route. That layered relationship helps a visitor inspect one object without losing their place in the broader set.

Related-item paths and the Models view provide the reverse move: from an individual character back to a scannable catalogue. The repeated circle makes that transition feel continuous even when the information density changes.

## Prototype outcome

The final Figma flow demonstrates a commerce system with two complementary readings. A visitor can browse campaigns and rooms for personality, or move through shop, product detail, and cart for clarity. The work resolves the interaction model and visual hierarchy; it does not claim a production launch, conversion result, or commercial outcome.
